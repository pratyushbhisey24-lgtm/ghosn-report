01 — Summary & Introduction

The Man Who Saved Cars — and Betrayed Them

"He was celebrated as a hero. He turned out to be a cautionary tale."

— Financial Times, 2019

Carlos Ghosn rose to global prominence as the man who rescued Nissan from near-bankruptcy in 1999, slashing costs, closing factories, and returning the company to profitability. By 2018 he was simultaneously CEO of Renault, Nissan, and Mitsubishi — an unprecedented automotive empire. Then, in November 2018, Japanese prosecutors arrested him on the tarmac of Tokyo's Haneda airport. The charges: years of financial misconduct, undisclosed compensation, and the systematic abuse of corporate power.

This report unmasks the psychological, cultural, and structural dynamics that allowed Ghosn's toxic leadership to flourish unchecked for nearly two decades. It applies frameworks from Hare & Babiak, Bakan, Harris, Kellerman, and Padilla et al. to examine both the leader and the followers who enabled him.

02 — Unethical Leadership

The Snake in the Boardroom

"Corporate psychopaths are not rare. They simply find that organisations provide an ideal habitat."

— Babiak & Hare, Snakes in Suits (2006)

Ghosn exhibited what Babiak and Hare (2006) describe as corporate psychopathy: a constellation of charm, grandiosity, calculated manipulation, and a fundamental absence of empathy or remorse. His public persona — the brilliant turnaround artist — was carefully constructed. Internally, former executives describe a culture of fear, surveillance, and retribution against dissent.

Harris (2021) distinguishes between transactional and transformational toxic leaders. Ghosn operated as both: transactionally, he tied employee incentives tightly to his personal approval; transformationally, he created a cult of personality that reframed ethical breaches as necessary sacrifices for corporate survival. This duality made him extraordinarily difficult to challenge.

Bakan's (2004) argument in The Corporation is critically relevant here: corporations are structurally designed to externalise costs and maximise returns regardless of human consequences. The alliance's governance structure, which concentrated power in a single figure while rewarding performance above all else, was the perfect habitat for Ghosn's psychology to flourish unchallenged.

03 — Problematic Followership

The Prey: Who Let the Snake Thrive?

"Followers are not innocent bystanders. Their complicity — active or passive — is the oxygen that keeps toxic leadership alive."

— Kellerman, Followership (2007)

Kellerman's (2007) follower typology maps precisely onto the Nissan case. The board of directors were classic bystanders — aware of anomalies, but choosing inaction out of deference, self-interest, or cultural pressure. Senior executives acted as participants, actively facilitating Ghosn's compensation schemes. Only a handful of insiders ultimately became activists — and it was their whistleblowing that finally triggered the arrest.

Padilla et al.'s (2007) toxic triangle model identifies three necessary conditions for destructive leadership: a destructive leader, susceptible followers, and a conducive environment. All three were present at Nissan. The alliance's cross-cultural structure — Japanese deference to authority meeting French top-down corporate culture — created an environment in which questioning Ghosn was structurally and culturally almost impossible.

The cultural dimension is crucial. In Japan, criticising a senior leader — especially a foreign saviour credited with the company's resurrection — carried extraordinary social cost. Employees and board members faced a double bind: speak and be ostracised, or stay silent and remain complicit. This is precisely the dynamic Kellerman describes as the "bystander effect" at an institutional scale.

04 — Recommendations

Breaking the Triangle: How Organisations Can Protect Themselves

The Ghosn case is not an anomaly — it is a system failure. The following recommendations are grounded in the theoretical frameworks examined throughout this report and designed to interrupt each side of Padilla et al.'s toxic triangle.

  • 01
    Separate the CEO and Chair roles — structurally
    Ghosn's simultaneous control of three boards made independent oversight impossible. Drawing on Bakan's (2004) call for "strong outside rules," corporate governance must mandate separation of executive and oversight functions, with board members drawn from diverse professional backgrounds.
  • 02
    Institutionalise psychological screening at the executive level
    Babiak and Hare (2006) demonstrate that corporate psychopaths actively exploit recruitment processes. Structured behavioural interviews, 360° reference checks, and ongoing board-level psychological assessment can surface Dark Triad traits before they metastasise into organisational culture.
  • 03
    Build a genuinely protected whistleblowing infrastructure
    Kellerman's (2007) activists — those who finally speak — need institutional protection. Anonymous reporting channels, external ombudspersons, and legal protections for whistleblowers must be embedded in governance frameworks, not merely listed in employee handbooks.
  • 04
    Cultivate critical followership as a cultural norm
    Harris (2021) argues that toxic leadership thrives in cultures that conflate loyalty with silence. Organisations must actively train followers in ethical challenge, normalising dissent at all levels and rewarding the raising of concerns rather than penalising it.
  • 05
    Mandate cross-cultural governance literacy in global alliances
    The Japanese-French cultural gap was a structural vulnerability. Global organisations must invest in cross-cultural governance training, ensuring that power imbalances embedded in national norms do not become mechanisms for abuse.

05 — Conclusion & Reflection

After the Escape: What the Ghosn Case Teaches Us

"The most dangerous leaders are not the ones who fail. They are the ones who succeed — until suddenly, catastrophically, they don't."

Carlos Ghosn's story is not primarily about one man's greed. It is about the conditions that allow toxic leadership to thrive: unchecked power, complicit followership, structurally captured governance, and a cultural mythology of the indispensable saviour. Each of the theoretical lenses applied in this report — Hare and Babiak's psychopathy framework, Bakan's corporate structure critique, Harris's typology, Kellerman's followership model, and Padilla et al.'s toxic triangle — illuminates a different facet of the same failure.

The lesson is not cynicism about leadership. It is about the design of systems that do not rely on the virtue of a single individual. Boards that ask hard questions. Employees who are protected when they raise concerns. Governance structures that distribute — rather than concentrate — power. These are not idealistic aspirations. They are learnable, buildable, organisational choices.
